# How children aged 8 to 12 tell and write stories

Research note for issue [#14](https://github.com/jimmyNicholas/narinav/issues/14).

**Question.** What does developmental and literacy research say about how children aged 8 to 12 tell and write stories: what they can typically do (structure, character, plot, endings, sentence-level writing), what they find hard, what they enjoy, and how the range differs across the band and for less confident writers?

**Scope.** What children can do and enjoy as storytellers. Learning design, curriculum mapping, UI and technical design are out of scope. The likely setting (short walk-up sessions at a festival or museum, adults nearby) was kept in mind when choosing what to read, but this note makes no design decisions.

**How this was researched (read this first).** Direct page fetches were blocked by the network proxy in this session, so every claim below was checked against search-engine excerpts of the cited source itself, not against a full-text read. Each claim links to the primary source (peer-reviewed paper, government or assessment body, or the National Literacy Trust). Where only a secondary write-up could be found, that is flagged. Anyone relying on a specific number should open the link and confirm it.

---

## 1. Summary

- **Structure.** By 8 most children can tell a story with a complete episode (a character, a problem, attempts, an outcome). Between 8 and 12 stories get more episodes, more causal links and more attention to what characters think and feel. By 11 to 12 typical children produce full story grammars linked within and across episodes.
- **Telling runs ahead of writing.** Children can say more than they can write. Handwriting and spelling are still not automatic at the bottom of the band and use up the working memory that would otherwise go on ideas and structure.
- **The biggest reported barrier is starting.** In a UK survey of 9,170 children aged 8 to 11, the top barrier to writing was "trouble deciding what to write" (45.5%), ahead of spelling and punctuation (34.7%).
- **Enjoyment is fragile and falling.** About two thirds of 8 to 11-year-olds said they enjoyed writing in 2017-18; by 2025 only about a quarter of 8 to 18-year-olds enjoyed writing in their free time. Choice of topic, inspiring prompts and familiar story worlds are the strongest reported motivators.
- **Less confident writers** plan very little, generate less content, struggle with transcription and stop early. Taking the transcription load away (for example by dictation) lets them produce more and better text.
- **Length and structure.** In about 30 minutes, fourth graders (about 9 to 10) wrote around 110 to 235 words on average, with a very wide range (60 words for low performers, up to 560 for one child). Between grades 4 and 6 stories got more complex but not longer. A 3-minute story from a prompt is a well-established short format up to grade 6. The structure children can reliably use is a simple problem and resolution (or orientation, complication and resolution). Teaching structure improves writing (effect size 0.59; strategy instruction 1.02), most of all for struggling writers, but researchers warn that rigid templates produce formulaic stories. I found no evidence that 8 to 12-year-olds use three-act, hero's journey or kishotenketsu structures.

## 2. What children can typically do

### 2.1 Story structure and plot

- Researchers using story-grammar analysis report a developmental trend in which children include more story-structure components as age increases from 3 to 9 (literature review in ["Age-Related Progressions in Story Structure in Young Children's Narratives", *JSLHR*, 2016](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED613909.pdf); [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1124080)). That study's own data cover ages 3 to 6, where tracking goals and subgoals was the hardest part.
- Five-year-olds tend to order stories around goals and plans, while 7 and 8-year-olds narrate stories with complete episodes; by ages 11 to 12 typically developing children produce full story grammars and connect sentences within and across episodes. This summary comes from the literature review in a 2023 story-grammar study ([Journal of Psycholinguistic Research, s10936-023-10007-y](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-023-10007-y)); treat it as a reviewed consensus rather than a single new finding.
- National assessment bodies expect the classic orientation, complication and resolution shape. Australia's NAPLAN narrative rubric (used in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9) scores "Text structure" as "the organisation of narrative features including orientation, complication and resolution", alongside separate criteria for Audience, Ideas, and Character and Setting ([ACARA NAPLAN narrative marking guide](https://www.nap.edu.au/docs/default-source/naplan/narrative-writing-marking-guide.pdf)).

### 2.2 Character, setting and inner states

- From about age 9, children's oral narratives include more characters' internal responses (emotions, motives, speech, causal explanation), and this grows into adolescence. This is a central finding of the cross-linguistic "frog story" studies that compared children aged 3, 4, 5 and 9 with adults ([Berman and Slobin, *Relating Events in Narrative*, 1994](https://www.perlego.com/book/1612217/relating-events-in-narrative-a-crosslinguistic-developmental-study-pdf); [project overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Slobin)). The second link is secondary and is given only for the project description.
- The English end of primary expectation (age 10 to 11) is that pupils "describe settings, characters and atmosphere" and "integrate dialogue in narratives to convey character and advance the action". Pupils "working towards" the standard "describe settings and characters" ([Standards and Testing Agency, KS2 teacher assessment framework, 2018-19](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/637ba0b0e90e072854bcab87/2018-19_teacher_assessment_frameworks_at_the_end_of_key_stage_2.pdf)).
- The Australian Curriculum (v9) frames the band as moving from borrowing to adapting: Year 3 students "create and edit imaginative texts, using or adapting language features, characters, settings, plot structures and ideas encountered in literary texts" (AC9E3LE05), and Year 6 students "create and edit literary texts that adapt plot structure, characters, settings and/or ideas from texts students have experienced, and experiment with literary devices" (AC9E6LE05) ([QCAA alignment of AC v9 Year 3](https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_yr3_as_cd_alignment.pdf); [Year 6](https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_yr6_as_cd_alignment.pdf); [Scootle AC9E6LE05](https://www.scootle.edu.au/ec/search?accContentId=AC9E6LE05)). Year 5 adds experimenting "with figurative language, storylines, characters and settings" (AC9E5LE05) ([QCAA Year 5](https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_yr5_as_cd_alignment.pdf)).

These are curriculum expectations, not measured outcomes, but they show what assessment bodies regard as typical across the band: stories built from and adapted from stories the child already knows.

### 2.3 Endings

- No high-trust primary study specifically on how 8 to 12-year-olds end stories was found in this session. National rubrics treat resolution as part of text structure ([ACARA NAPLAN narrative marking guide](https://www.nap.edu.au/docs/default-source/naplan/narrative-writing-marking-guide.pdf)), and writing-development research describes progress as, for example, "a longer story that includes a significant complication and a satisfying resolution" ([Sandiford and Macken-Horarik, "Changing stories", *Assessing Writing*, 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1075293520300325)), which implies satisfying resolutions are a later achievement.
- Teacher commentary (secondary, low trust) describes stock shortcuts such as "and then I woke up" when children run out of time or trap their hero ([Tes archive](https://www.tes.com/magazine/archive/end-full-stop-thats-it)). **Gap:** worth a targeted follow-up if endings matter to the design.

### 2.4 Sentence-level writing

- Syntactic complexity in written narratives shows a clear developmental progression between grades 4 and 6 (about ages 9 to 12), even where text length and story content did not increase with age; executive functions predicted that growth ([Drijbooms, Groen and Verhoeven, *Reading and Writing*, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5247531/)).
- In a study of 102 Dutch fourth graders (about age 9 to 10), inhibition and updating (working memory) contributed directly to story length, and indirectly, through handwriting, to length, syntactic complexity and story content ([Drijbooms, Groen and Verhoeven, *Reading and Writing*, 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4503883/); [ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1068956)).
- Australian national data show most children sit in the middle of the sentence-level scales: in a decade of NAPLAN writing, most students in Year 3 (79%), Year 5 (75%) and Year 7 (59%) scored 2 out of 4 on the relevant criterion, and top scores for Audience, Text structure and Sentence structure were rare ([AERO, *Writing development: what does a decade of NAPLAN data reveal?*, summary](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED673992.pdf); [full report](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED673991.pdf)). Note the report focuses mainly on persuasive rather than narrative writing.
- In the US, 2011 NAEP found that about three quarters of grade 8 students (about age 13 to 14, just above the band) wrote at or above Basic but only about a quarter at or above Proficient (24% Proficient, 3% Advanced) ([NAEP 2011 Writing summary](https://www.nationsreportcard.gov/writing_2011/summary.asp)). Even at the top of the band, crafted, controlled prose is the exception, not the norm.

## 3. What they find hard

### 3.1 Getting started and generating ideas

- Among 9,170 UK pupils aged 8 to 11, the most common barriers were "having trouble deciding what to write" (45.5%), "struggling with spelling and punctuation" (34.7%) and "finding writing in itself difficult" (19.4%) ([National Literacy Trust, *Writing enjoyment, behaviours and attitudes in 8 to 11-year-olds in 2017-18*](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/writing-enjoyment-research-2017-18/); [PDF](https://literacytrust.org.uk/documents/1649/Writing_enjoyment_attitudes_and_behaviours_in_8_to_11-year-olds_in_2017-18_.pdf)).

### 3.2 Holding the whole story in mind

- Young writers typically use "knowledge telling": writing down ideas as they come to mind, driven by "what to say next" rather than how each idea fits the whole text or the reader. Mature writers shift to "knowledge transforming" ([Bereiter and Scardamalia's model as summarised in ETS, "Cognitive Models of Writing"](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1111278.pdf); [*Kinds of knowledge-telling*, Journal of Writing Research](https://www.jowr.org/jowr/article/view/723)).
- In children aged 8 to 11, working memory and shifting account for variance in plot complexity in written narratives (reported in the review section of [Scionti, Zampini and Marzocchi, *Children*, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10453360/)). The same meta-analysis (30 studies) found executive functions and narrative skill only weakly associated overall (r = 0.236), more strongly before age 7 and more independent after.

### 3.3 Transcription: handwriting and spelling

- Handwriting and spelling are related to composing across grades 1 to 6; in grades 1 to 3 they together explained 25% of the variance in composition quality and 66% in fluency (Graham et al., 1997, reported in [Kent et al. via PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3474373/) and [Graham, Harris and Fink-Chorzempa, *Journal of Special Education*, 2005](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ693936.pdf)).
- The EEF's Key Stage 2 guidance (ages 7 to 11) uses the "Simple View of Writing" (text generation, transcription, executive functions) and says handwriting, typing, spelling and sentence construction need to become increasingly automatic so pupils can concentrate on composition ([EEF, *Improving Literacy in Key Stage 2*, 2nd edition](https://d2tic4wvo1iusb.cloudfront.net/production/eef-guidance-reports/literacy-ks2/EEF-Improving-literacy-in-key-stage-2-report-Second-edition.pdf); [EEF page](https://educationendowmentfoundation.org.uk/education-evidence/guidance-reports/literacy-ks2)).
- Children who type faster and make more small online revisions write better and longer stories ([*The dynamics of narrative writing in primary grade children*, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4761367/)).
- Typing is not automatically easier across the band. Most studies in grades 1 to 9 find handwriting faster than keyboarding, though some find grade 6 students type faster; one finding is that narratives were higher quality on paper for grades 4 to 5 but the reverse for grades 6 to 7 as typing skill grew ([Keyboarding and handwriting, *Reading and Writing*, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-025-10694-0); [Underlying functions associated with keyboarding, *Scand J Occ Ther*, 2023](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/11038128.2023.2188254)).

### 3.4 Telling versus writing

- Children routinely produce more text when dictating than when writing, and transcription limits the length of written texts but not their structural organisation; dictation did not change the quality produced by most average and good writers (Scardamalia, Bereiter and Goelman, 1982, "The role of production factors in writing ability", as summarised in [De La Paz and Graham, 1997](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effects-of-dictation-and-advanced-planning-instruction-on-the-com/) and [Semantic Scholar record](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Effects-of-dictation-and-advanced-planning-on-the-Paz-Graham/c25e9580dd792e9ad79b3550dcf27ececf58da72); the original chapter was not reachable online).
- In grades 3 and 5, children asked to continue a story were more fluent with narrative than expository text in both speech and writing, and spoken continuations tended to be longer ([Comparison of oral and written productions in two discourse types](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/254301899_The_comparison_of_oral_and_written_productions_in_two_discourse_types)).

## 4. What they enjoy

- **Most younger children in the band start positive.** In 2017-18, 68.5% of UK 8 to 11-year-olds said they enjoyed writing; 65.9% thought writing was fun ([NLT 2017-18](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/writing-enjoyment-research-2017-18/)).
- **But enjoyment is falling and drops with age.** In 2025 only 26.6% of 8 to 18-year-olds enjoyed writing in their free time, down 20.2 percentage points since 2010, with primary-aged children dropping 6 points in a single year ([NLT, *Children and young people's writing in 2025*](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-writing-in-2025/)). In 2024 the figure was 28.7%, with the steepest one-year fall among boys aged 5 to 8 ([NLT, *Writing in 2024*](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-writing-in-2024/)). Earlier UK government review: more girls than boys, and more younger than older pupils, enjoy writing ([DfE, *What is the research evidence on writing?*, RR238, 2012](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7b1474ed915d429748cadf/DFE-RR238.pdf)).
- **Choice matters.** About 86% of pupils said they liked to choose what they write about ([DfE RR238](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7b1474ed915d429748cadf/DFE-RR238.pdf), citing NLT). Analysing open responses from 55,075 8 to 18-year-olds, NLT found that even children averse to writing named autonomy (choosing their own topic or format), inspiring prompts and writing about personal experience as what would re-engage them; keen writers stay engaged when writing feels meaningful, expressive, "cognitively manageable" and socially supported, with an authentic audience ([NLT, *Why children and young people do or do not engage with writing in their free time*](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/why-children-and-young-people-do-or-do-not-engage-with-writing-in-their-free-time/)).
- **Familiar story worlds.** Children build their stories out of stories they already know: in an ethnography of 7 to 9-year-olds, superhero and other media stories were central to their fiction and play ([Dyson, *Writing Superheroes*, 1997, ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED461101)). The curriculum expectations in 2.2 make the same assumption (adapting characters, settings and plots from known texts).
- **Humour.** From about 7 children understand and enjoy jokes and riddles that depend on double meanings, the stage that runs to about 11 in McGhee's account of humour development; in one study laughter at such humour peaked at age 11 ([Humor development in preschool and primary children, ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1382690.pdf)).
- **Telling with others.** Peer-oriented storytelling and story-acting improved narrative skill, and child storytellers, actors and adults co-construct stories through gaze, gesture and speech ([Nicolopoulou, "Peer-group culture and narrative development", 2002](https://lchcautobio.ucsd.edu/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/Nicolopoulou-2002-Peer-Group-Culture-and-Narrative-Development.pdf); [Cremin et al., *Journal of Early Childhood Research*, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1177/1476718x17750205)). **Caveat:** this work is with children under 8; no comparable primary study for 8 to 12 was found in this session.

## 5. How the range differs

### 5.1 Across the band (8 to 12)

| Aspect | Around 8 to 9 | Around 11 to 12 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Structure | Complete single episodes; additive "and then" sequences common | Multiple linked episodes; full story grammar ([J Psycholinguist Res review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-023-10007-y)) |
| Character | Actions and simple feelings | More inner states, motives, dialogue that conveys character ([KS2 framework](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/637ba0b0e90e072854bcab87/2018-19_teacher_assessment_frameworks_at_the_end_of_key_stage_2.pdf)) |
| Sentences | Simpler syntax; transcription still effortful | Clear growth in syntactic complexity grades 4 to 6 ([Drijbooms et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5247531/)) |
| Source of ideas | Using and adapting known stories (AC9E3LE05) | Adapting and experimenting with literary devices (AC9E6LE05) |
| Typing | Usually slower than handwriting | Some catch up or overtake ([Reading and Writing 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11145-025-10694-0)) |
| Enjoyment | Higher, but falling fastest recently | Lower; declines with age ([NLT 2025](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/children-and-young-peoples-writing-in-2025/)) |

Growth is uneven: development in writing "is not neat and can be uneven", showing in one area (a longer story with a real complication and resolution) while another stalls ([Sandiford and Macken-Horarik, 2020](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1075293520300325)). Age is a weak guide to any one child.

### 5.2 Less confident and struggling writers

- Students who struggle with writing, especially those with learning disabilities, typically have limited knowledge of writing, do little advance planning, have difficulty generating content, rarely revise for meaning, struggle with transcription, show low persistence and may have an unrealistic sense of their own ability. Grade 5 and 6 students with mild learning disabilities averaged less than one minute of planning ([Graham, Harris and colleagues, *Learning Disabilities: A Contemporary Journal*, 2007](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ797667.pdf); ["Why is writing so difficult for students with learning disabilities?", ERIC](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1039858.pdf)).
- For grade 5 to 7 students with writing and learning problems, combining dictation with planning support produced more complete and better texts ([De La Paz and Graham, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 1997](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effects-of-dictation-and-advanced-planning-instruction-on-the-com/)).
- One quarter to one third of students with slow, less legible handwriting produced more text with a keyboard once proficient ([Keyboarding for students with handwriting problems](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/7973283_Keyboarding_for_Students_with_Handwriting_Problems)).
- Confidence and enjoyment travel together: barriers such as not knowing what to write and spelling worries are more pronounced among children who don't enjoy writing ([NLT 2017-18](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/writing-enjoyment-research-2017-18/)), and averse writers describe "cognitive overload" and writing feeling misaligned with who they are ([NLT engagement report](https://literacytrust.org.uk/research-services/research-reports/why-children-and-young-people-do-or-do-not-engage-with-writing-in-their-free-time/)).
- Gender: girls outperform boys in writing (for example 87% vs 76% at the expected level in the 2012 English data) and more girls enjoy writing ([DfE RR238](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7b1474ed915d429748cadf/DFE-RR238.pdf)).

## 6. Story length and story structures

### 6.1 Story length

**Written length in a timed task.** The best large-sample numbers come from national assessments, where children get about 25 to 40 minutes per story:

- **Age 9 to 10, about 30 minutes.** In the 2012 NAEP computer-based writing pilot (about 10,400 US fourth graders, roughly 30 minutes per task, covering stories as well as persuasive and explanatory writing), the average response was **110 words** typed, against **159 words** on the 2010 paper pilot. Low performers wrote about **60** words, middle performers about **104** and high performers about **179** ([NCES, *Performance of fourth-grade students in the 2012 NAEP computer-based writing pilot assessment*, 2015](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/subject/writing/pdf/2015119.pdf); [NCES record](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/pubs/writing/2015119.aspx)). For timing, see the [NAEP 2011 framework](https://www.nagb.gov/content/dam/nagb/en/documents/publications/frameworks/writing/2011-writing-framework.pdf) (two 25-minute paper tasks at grade 4, two 30-minute tasks at grades 8 and 12) and the [NAEP 2017 technical summary](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/subject/writing/pdf/2017_writing_technical_summary.pdf) (about 30 minutes per task at grade 4).
- **Age 9 to 10, picture-prompted story.** 102 Dutch fourth graders writing a story from a picture sequence averaged about **235 words** (SD about 103), ranging from **75 to 560** ([Drijbooms, Groen and Verhoeven, 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4503883/)). A fivefold range in one class is the clearest single piece of evidence on how wide the band is.
- **Grades 4 to 6.** Following the same children, story length and story content did **not** grow between grades 4 and 6, while sentence complexity did ([Drijbooms, Groen and Verhoeven, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5247531/)). Getting older made the stories more complex rather than longer.
- **Australia.** The NAPLAN writing test gives about 40 minutes in Year 3 and 42 minutes in Years 5, 7 and 9, split roughly into planning, writing and editing (secondary summary: [naplanwriting.com.au](https://naplanwriting.com.au/how-naplan-works), **flagged as not an official source**). I found no official ACARA figure for typical script length.

**Written length in a very short task (under about 15 minutes).** The best-studied short format is written-expression curriculum-based measurement (CBM). The child gets a story starter, thinks for 1 minute, then writes for 3 minutes. It is described as a reliable and valid measure of story writing for typically achieving students through grade 6 and for students with severe writing difficulties ([Wright, *How To: Track Growth in Written Expression*, Intervention Central](https://www.interventioncentral.org/assessment/writing_assessment_elementary), a practitioner summary of the CBM research, **secondary**; underlying norms in Malecki and Jewell, *Psychology in the Schools*, 2003, [doi:10.1002/pits.10096](https://doi.org/10.1002/pits.10096)). This shows children aged 8 to 12 can start and produce a short story in a few minutes from a prompt. I could not confirm the actual words-per-3-minutes norms from a primary source in this session, so none are quoted here.

**What children can hold in mind.** I found no study that measures directly how many sentences or episodes children can keep track of. The nearest evidence:

- By ages 11 to 12, typical children connect sentences within and across several episodes. At 7 to 8 the typical unit is one complete episode ([review in *J Psycholinguist Res*, 2023](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10936-023-10007-y)).
- Young writers write "what comes next" rather than planning the whole text ([Bereiter and Scardamalia's knowledge-telling model, ETS summary](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1111278.pdf)). Working memory and shifting explain differences in plot complexity at ages 8 to 11 ([Scionti et al., 2023, review section](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10453360/)).

**Oral telling.** Children produce more words when telling or dictating than when writing. Transcription limits length but not structure (Scardamalia, Bereiter and Goelman, 1982, as summarised in [De La Paz and Graham, 1997](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effects-of-dictation-and-advanced-planning-instruction-on-the-com/)). In oral narrative samples, total words, number of different words and words per minute all rise with age from 3 to 13 (r about .65 to .72), and clinicians treat a retell of 35 to 65 utterances as a stable sample ([SALT Software reference book](https://www.saltsoftware.com/media/wysiwyg/SALT%20Reference%20Book.pdf), a clinical assessment publisher, **secondary**). The ENNI oral narrative norms only reach age 9 ([ENNI database documentation](https://www.saltsoftware.com/media/wysiwyg/reference_database/ENNIRDBDoc.pdf)), so I found no oral length norms for 10 to 12.

**Less confident writers.** In the NAEP pilot, low performers wrote about a third as much as high performers (60 vs 179 words) ([NCES 2015-119](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/subject/writing/pdf/2015119.pdf)). Struggling writers in grades 5 and 6 planned for under a minute and showed low persistence ([Graham, Harris and colleagues, 2007](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ797667.pdf)). When dictation removed the transcription load and planning was supported, they wrote more complete texts ([De La Paz and Graham, 1997](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/effects-of-dictation-and-advanced-planning-instruction-on-the-com/)). Typing is not automatically shorter or longer: the NAEP fourth graders wrote fewer words on computer than on paper ([NCES 2015-119](https://nces.ed.gov/nationsreportcard/subject/writing/pdf/2015119.pdf)).

### 6.2 Named story structures: what children are taught and already know

| Structure | Evidence it is taught to or known by 8 to 12-year-olds | Source quality |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orientation, complication, resolution (optional coda) | The structure scored by Australia's national test in Years 3, 5, 7 and 9 ([ACARA NAPLAN narrative marking guide](https://www.nap.edu.au/docs/default-source/naplan/narrative-writing-marking-guide.pdf)). It comes from Australian genre pedagogy (Martin, Rothery), which is built into Australian syllabuses ([Caldwell and White, 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315665684_That's_not_a_narrative_this_is_a_narrative_NAPLAN_and_pedagogies_of_storytelling)) | Primary (assessment body; peer-reviewed paper) |
| Problem and resolution, or story grammar (character, setting, goal or problem, attempts, outcome, feelings) | The basis of story-grammar research (section 2.1). Over half of narrative-language interventions directly teach character, problem and solution ([review, *LSHSS*, 2021](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1317773.pdf)). The SRSD story strategy asks Who, When, Where; What does the main character do and what happens; How does it end and how does the character feel ([SRSD "WWW, What=2, How=2" figure](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/POW-WWW-What-2-How-2-Story-Writing-Framework-and-Mnemonic_fig1_257643700)) | Primary (peer-reviewed review); figure via ResearchGate |
| Story mountain; five parts (opening, build-up, problem, resolution, ending) | England's former National Strategies taught storytelling with story maps and mountains, a five-part structure and named plots (warning tale, wishing tale, quest, defeating the monster, lost and found). Children were to learn a bank of about 50 stories orally across primary school ([Corbett, *Storytelling*, DCSF National Strategies](https://www.foundationyears.org.uk/files/2011/10/Story-Teling_Story-Making1.pdf); [Talk for Writing generic plots](https://www.talk4writing.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/08/Adapted_Generic_Story_Plots_MR2.pdf)) | Government guidance (practice, not research); Talk for Writing is a practitioner source |
| Beginning, middle, end | Widely used in classrooms, but I found no high-trust source defining it for this age band | Gap |
| Three-act structure | No evidence found that it is taught to, or used by, 8 to 12-year-olds | Gap |
| Hero's journey | One small naturalistic study used it for shared reading and response in a Year 6 class, not as a writing structure ([*The Hero's Journey: personal resonance as response to narrative*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324388387_'The_Hero's_Journey'_Personal_Resonance_as_Response_to_Narrative)) | Weak; gap |
| Kishotenketsu (introduction, development, twist, conclusion; no conflict needed) | Described as widespread in Japanese storytelling, including children's stories ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kish%C5%8Dtenketsu), **secondary**). I found no research on English-speaking 8 to 12-year-olds using it | Secondary only; gap |

**Which ones children can actually use.** The evidence supports the simple problem and resolution shape. Most 8-year-olds manage one complete episode, and the oldest in the band link several (section 2.1). Curriculum documents at both ends of the band expect children to borrow and adapt plots, characters and settings from stories they already know, rather than invent structures from scratch (AC9E3LE05, AC9E6LE05; [QCAA Year 3](https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_yr3_as_cd_alignment.pdf), [Year 6](https://www.qcaa.qld.edu.au/downloads/aciqv9/english/curriculum/ac9_english_yr6_as_cd_alignment.pdf)). I found no evidence that children in this band can use more elaborate frameworks (three-act, hero's journey, kishotenketsu) in their own writing.

### 6.3 Scaffolding with a structure versus free writing

- **Teaching structure helps on average.** In a meta-analysis of writing instruction in grades 1 to 6, teaching text structure improved writing quality (effect size 0.59). Strategy instruction scored 1.02 and the SRSD model 1.17. Prewriting activities scored 0.54, peer help while writing 0.89, product goals 0.76 and creativity or imagery instruction 0.70. A general "process approach" with more open writing scored 0.40 ([Graham, McKeown, Kiuhara and Harris, *Journal of Educational Psychology*, 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ994038); [ASU record](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/a-meta-analysis-of-writing-instruction-for-students-in-the-elemen/)). These are learning effects over weeks of teaching, not effects within one session, so they only show that structure helps children write better stories. They are not an outcome target.
- **Struggling writers gain most from explicit structure.** The What Works Clearinghouse rated SRSD "potentially positive" for students aged 7 to 16 with learning disabilities: 15 of 17 single-case experiments showed a positive effect and none a negative one ([WWC SRSD intervention report, 2017](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/InterventionReports/wwc_srsd_111417.pdf)).
- **Over-rigid structure has costs.** Australian researchers argue that NAPLAN's focus on complication and resolution steers students away from other effective ways of telling a story. They say it produces "wooden and narrow" formulaic narratives, and that scoring of stories which do not fit the template is inconsistent ([Caldwell and White, *Australian Journal of Language and Literacy*, 2017](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315665684_That's_not_a_narrative_this_is_a_narrative_NAPLAN_and_pedagogies_of_storytelling); see also [*Re-imagining narrative writing and assessment*, 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44020-022-00004-4)). This is argument and analysis, not a controlled comparison.
- **Free choice drives enjoyment.** The motivation evidence in section 4 (choosing one's own topic, inspiring prompts) is about choosing content, not dropping structure. I found no study that directly compares structured and free storytelling for enjoyment at ages 8 to 12.

## 7. Points relevant to Narinav (observations, not decisions)

These link the findings to the context in the ticket. They are for later decision tickets to weigh.

- A one-sentence-at-a-time turn matches "knowledge telling" (deciding what comes next) and keeps each turn small, which the research suggests is cognitively manageable for the bottom of the band. Keeping the whole story coherent is the part children find hardest, so it is where a partner would carry most of the load.
- The biggest reported barrier is not knowing what to write. A blank start is the riskiest moment in a short walk-up session.
- Children draw heavily on stories, characters and humour they already know, and value choosing their own topic.
- Spelling and typing effort vary a lot across 8 to 12 and are worst for less confident writers; speaking a sentence is typically easier than writing it.
- Satisfying endings are a later achievement; children may want help landing one.
- Not decisions: average written stories at ages 9 to 10 are about 100 to 250 words in half an hour, with low performers nearer 60, so a short session will produce a short story unless the child's writing load is reduced. Telling is longer than writing.
- Not decisions: the structure with the strongest evidence for this age is simple problem and resolution, built from characters and plots children already know. Explicit structure helps struggling writers most, while research warns that a fixed template flattens stories.

## 8. Gaps and confidence

- **Fetch limitation:** all sources were checked through search excerpts, not full reads (see top of note). Specific figures should be confirmed at the link before being quoted elsewhere.
- **Endings:** no primary study found on how 8 to 12-year-olds resolve stories.
- **Collaborative storytelling with a partner (human or AI) at ages 8 to 12:** the primary evidence found is with under-8s.
- **Short informal sessions:** almost all evidence comes from classrooms and tests, where children write longer pieces under time and assessment pressure. How this transfers to a short, voluntary festival or museum session is unknown.
- **Length norms:** I found no primary source giving 3-minute CBM word counts, oral story length norms for ages 10 to 12, or a direct measure of how many sentences or episodes a child can hold in mind.
- **Named structures:** I found no research on 8 to 12-year-olds using beginning-middle-end as a defined structure, or the three-act, hero's journey or kishotenketsu structures. The story mountain evidence is government practice guidance, not research.
- **Structure versus free writing:** the effect sizes come from teaching over weeks, not single sessions. I found no direct comparison of enjoyment.
- **Population:** sources are mainly UK, Australian, US and Dutch. Children writing in an additional language are not covered.
