# How do player-directed story games work?

Research for [issue #15](https://github.com/jimmyNicholas/narinav/issues/15). Surveyed 4 October 2026.

## How claims were verified

The network egress proxy blocked every direct page fetch tried (Wikipedia, bay12games.com, gamedeveloper.com, thinkygames.com, electricliterature.com, blog.zarfhome.com, wileywiggins.com). **No source in this file was opened and read in full.** Each claim was checked against web-search result snippets, which quote or paraphrase the linked page. Source tags used below:

- **[primary]**: the designer, publisher, official rulebook or store page, or a peer-reviewed or academic paper.
- **[secondary]**: journalism, reviews, fan wikis, community guides, Wikipedia. Used where no primary source surfaced, or to corroborate.

## Summary

- Across every example, the **player directs** and the **game supplies constraint and record**: a limited set of doors, three drafted rooms, a dice-picked prompt, a yes/no oracle, a list of tasks that autonomous characters may or may not carry out.
- **The story is mostly made by the player, not delivered to them.** Academic work on emergent narrative (Ryan; Kreminski and Mateas) argues the raw log of play is not yet a story; the player curates and embellishes it into one. Games that produce memorable stories give the player a good record to curate from.
- **Tracking devices split three ways**: the game keeps the record (Dwarf Fortress Legends, 80 Days' journal), the player keeps it (Blue Prince's lack of a journal, Maze's paper-and-pencil solving), or the record is itself a constrained game piece (Thousand Year Old Vampire's five Memories and diary).
- **Suggestions are almost always a small, finite menu**: 2 to 4 doors, 3 drafted rooms, 3 prompts per page, 5 odds bands. The thing that keeps the story moving is not fewer choices but a **budget** (steps, days, dice, an 80-day clock) that forces commitment.
- **Length**: runs range from one sitting (an 80 Days trip of about 1 to 4 hours, a Blue Prince day) to open-ended campaigns (Dwarf Fortress worlds, Ironsworn). Endings are usually **failure-tolerant**: a lost fortress, a day out of steps or a dead vampire is a valid end that still yields a story.

## Precedents

### Christopher Manson, *Maze* (1985)

Why included: named in the working concept; also the acknowledged ancestor of Blue Prince.

- **What it is.** A puzzle book written and illustrated by Manson, published by Henry Holt in 1985. Each page is a room in a house; each room leads to other rooms. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze:_Solve_the_World%27s_Most_Challenging_Puzzle)] Manson described it as "not really a book... a building in the shape of a book". [secondary, quoting Manson: [zoran rosko blog](https://zorosko.blogspot.com/2019/02/christopher-manson-this-is-not-really.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze:_Solve_the_World%27s_Most_Challenging_Puzzle)]
- **Who directs.** The reader. Each illustrated room has numbered doors; the reader picks one and turns to that page. [secondary: [intotheabyss.net](http://www.intotheabyss.net/); [Electric Literature](https://electricliterature.com/a-labyrinth-in-the-shape-of-a-book/)]
- **Who responds.** An unnamed Guide narrates in the second person: the reader is part of a group being led through the house, and the Guide is unreliable and "less-than-helpful", winking at his own plans. [secondary: [Electric Literature](https://electricliterature.com/a-labyrinth-in-the-shape-of-a-book/); [jeffreysomers.com](https://www.jeffreysomers.com/blather/maze-by-christopher-manson/)]
- **What it tracks.** Nothing. The book has no state; any record (paths taken, clues noticed) is the reader's own.
- **What it offers next.** The doors visible in the picture, plus the Guide's text, which may mislead.
- **How a story comes out.** There is a goal (reach Room 45 and return to Room 1 in 16 steps, decode the riddle at the centre, find its answer along the shortest path) [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze:_Solve_the_World%27s_Most_Challenging_Puzzle); [intotheabyss.net](http://www.intotheabyss.net/the-solution-to-mansons-maze-the-16-step-path/)], but the narrative is the reader's wandering and the Guide's running commentary on it. The book was a contest with a US$10,000 prize; nobody solved it before the contest closed in September 1987, and the prize was split among the closest entrants. The contest was the publisher's idea, not Manson's. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maze:_Solve_the_World%27s_Most_Challenging_Puzzle); [intotheabyss.net history](http://www.intotheabyss.net/history-of-maze/)]
- **Length and ending.** Open: 45 rooms, loops allowed, and no forced end. The "ending" is the reader choosing to stop or solving the puzzle. Solving was a decades-long community effort (the intotheabyss.net site documents it). [secondary: [intotheabyss.net](http://www.intotheabyss.net/)]

### *Blue Prince* (Dogubomb, 2025)

Why included: named in the working concept; a direct, credited descendant of *Maze*.

- **Lineage.** Creator Tonda Ros was inspired by *Maze* as a child; Manson contributed minor parts to the game. [secondary, interview: [Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/blue-prince-interview-tonda-ros-no-dlc-maze-christopher-manson-intotheabyss-white-raven); [Invent with Python blog](https://inventwithpython.com/blog/the-maze-online.html)] The game began as a concept for "a physical tabletop game involving drawing cards and drafting rooms". [secondary, quoting Ros: [Thinky Games interview](https://thinkygames.com/features/interview-how-myst-riven-and-tabletop-games-built-the-foundation-of-blue-prince/)]
- **Who directs.** The player, as Simon P. Jones, who inherits Mt. Holly on condition he finds a hidden 46th room. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Prince_(video_game))]
- **What it offers next.** Opening a door asks the player to draft one of **three** randomly offered floor plans into the adjacent space. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Prince)] Some rooms cost gems; some doors need keys. [secondary: [Adventure Game Hotspot review](https://adventuregamehotspot.com/review/4117/blue-prince)]
- **What it tracks.** Within a day: steps, gems, keys, the layout drafted so far. Across days: a small set of permanent upgrades; inventory resets each day. [secondary: [QuestDuo](https://questduo.com/blue-prince/blue-prince-all-permanent-upgrades/); [Steam guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3477468815)] Notably, the game **does not keep an in-game journal** of documents read; the player is expected to take their own notes. [secondary: [Adventure Game Hotspot review](https://adventuregamehotspot.com/review/4117/blue-prince); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Prince)]
- **How a story comes out.** Two layers. Each day produces a one-off house and a small tale of what was drafted and found. Across days, the player pieces together authored lore (the Sinclair family, the disappearance of author Marion Marigold) from fragments found in rooms. [secondary: [Game Rant](https://gamerant.com/blue-prince-story-immersion-how/); [Vice interview](https://www.vice.com/en/article/my-interview-with-the-master-of-mayhem-himself-tonda-ros-creator-of-blue-prince/)]
- **Length and ending.** A day lasts until the player spends 50 steps (one per room entered) or chooses to end it; the house rearranges overnight. [secondary: [GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/gallery/blue-prince-tips-beginners-guide-things-to-know/2900-6444/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Prince_(video_game))] Reaching Room 46 rolls credits, but a long post-game continues, with puzzles unsolved by the community months after release. [secondary: [Steam guide](https://steamcommunity.com/sharedfiles/filedetails/?id=3477468815); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Prince)]

### *Dwarf Fortress* (Bay 12 Games, 2006 onward): fortress mode and Legends mode

Why included: named in the working concept; the most-studied example of emergent narrative.

- **Who directs (fortress mode).** The player controls the expedition **indirectly**: they designate work and set standing orders, and dwarves carry it out with some autonomy. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dwarf_Fortress); [DF Wiki: Standing orders](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/Standing_orders)] Tarn Adams describes the player as the "official will of the fortress" while dwarves keep autonomy outside their duties, "allowing them to be actors in their own stories", which he names as a large part of where emergent narrative comes from. [primary, Adams interview; attribution from search snippet, not fetched: [Game Developer Q&A](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-dissecting-the-development-of-i-dwarf-fortress-i-with-creator-tarn-adams)]
- **What it tracks.** A great deal. Adams's chapter says that even the 2006 release gave each dwarf a name, skills, sex, age, family, position, likes and dislikes, an injurable body and items, and that characterisation increased players' ability and enthusiasm to tell stories. [primary: Adams, "Emergent Narrative in Dwarf Fortress", in Short and Adams (eds), *[Procedural Storytelling in Game Design](https://www.routledge.com/Procedural-Storytelling-in-Game-Design/Short-Adams/p/book/9781138595309)*, CRC Press 2019; [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/356686095_Characterization_and_Emergent_Narrative_in_Dwarf_Fortress)] Events in a fortress are "recorded for all time in the annals of your world", and dwarves' engravings depict those events. [secondary: [DF Wiki: Fortress mode](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Fortress_mode); [DF Wiki: Engrave](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/40d:Engrave)]
- **Legends mode** is a read-only view of a generated world: maps, the histories of each civilisation, every figure who lived or died, and any notable achievement from the player's own fortress or adventure. History can be exported to text and XML. [secondary: [DF Wiki: Legends](http://dwarffortresswiki.org/legends); [DF Wiki: World History file](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:World_History_file)] It is a tracking device in its purest form: a queryable record that offers no next move.
- **What it offers next.** Not suggestions as such; the simulation produces situations (sieges, moods, beasts) that demand a response.
- **How a story comes out.** Through player retelling. *Boatmurdered*, a Something Awful forum game in which players took turns running one fortress for a year each, is widely credited with showing the game's emergent storytelling and popularising the Let's Play form. [secondary: [Wikipedia: Boatmurdered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boatmurdered); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/the-eight-best-dwarf-fortress-stories-weve-seen-1833893569)]
- **Length and ending.** Fortresses run for in-game years over many sessions. The motto is "Losing is fun!"; a lost or abandoned fortress can be reclaimed by a later expedition, and its history persists in the world. [secondary: [Wikipedia: Boatmurdered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boatmurdered); [DF Wiki: Fortress mode](https://dwarffortresswiki.org/index.php/DF2014:Fortress_mode)]

### *Thousand Year Old Vampire* (Tim Hutchings, 2019)

Why included: named in the working concept; the best-known solo journaling RPG.

- **Who directs.** The player, writing a vampire's life as a journal. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thousand_Year_Old_Vampire)]
- **What it offers next.** About 80 pages of prompts, three per page. Each turn the player rolls d10 minus d6 and moves forward or back that many prompts. (The rule for which of a page's three prompts applies was not verified here.) [secondary: [RPGnet review](https://www.rpg.net/reviews/archive/18/18890.phtml); [Rolling Boxcars](https://rollingboxcars.com/2020/11/09/unlife-of-memories-thousand-year-old-vampire/)] Prompts grant Skills, ask questions to journal as Experiences, or order the vampire to kill a Character, use a Skill or lose a Resource. [secondary: [Bumbling Through Dungeons](https://bumblingthroughdungeons.com/thousand-year-old-vampire/)]
- **What it tracks.** Skills, Resources, Characters and **Memories**: at most five, each holding up to three Experiences. Adding a Memory when full means forgetting one. Up to four Memories may be moved into a Diary, which the vampire cannot recall from, and which can itself be lost. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thousand_Year_Old_Vampire); [Rolling Boxcars](https://rollingboxcars.com/2020/11/09/unlife-of-memories-thousand-year-old-vampire/)] Hutchings's design theme is choosing what to remember and what to forget. [secondary, profile: [Heart of the Deernicorn](https://heartofthedeernicorn.com/blog/tim-hutchings-is-making-games-about-forgetting/)]
- **How a story comes out.** The journal is the story. Because the record is bounded, coherence is traded for drift on purpose: early events fall away and the character changes.
- **Length and ending.** A few hours to a few weeks; reviewers report 1 to 4 hour sessions across one or several sittings. Play ends when a prompt brings the vampire's end and the player narrates it. [secondary: [Tabletop Bookshelf](https://tabletopbookshelf.com/products/thousand-year-old-vampire); [Bumbling Through Dungeons](https://bumblingthroughdungeons.com/thousand-year-old-vampire/)]

### Additional example 1: *Ironsworn* (Shawn Tomkin, 2018)

Why chosen: the strongest example of a **solo** game whose job is to answer the player's questions rather than lead them. It is close to the "player directs, game offers possibilities" shape, and the full rulebook is free.

- **Who directs.** The player, as a character bound by vows. [primary: [Ironsworn on itch.io](https://shawn-tomkin.itch.io/ironsworn); [Tomkin Press](https://tomkinpress.com/)]
- **What it offers next.** Oracles. *Ask the Oracle* lets the player set odds (Almost Certain, Likely, 50/50, Unlikely, Small Chance) on a yes/no question and roll d100; doubles add an extreme result or a twist. For "what happens next", roll on Action and Theme tables for a two-word spark to interpret. [primary text via secondary reproductions: [Chartopia: Ask the Oracle](https://chartopia.d12dev.com/chart/2054/); [Ironsworn rulebook (anyflip)](https://anyflip.com/ibdok/ebfi/basic)]
- **What it tracks.** Character stats, assets, and **progress tracks** for vows; finishing a vow earns experience. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironsworn)]
- **How a story comes out.** The player narrates around the mechanics; the rules give "abundant guidance" so the solo game can lean toward journaling or toward a traditional RPG. [secondary: [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ironsworn)]
- **Length and ending.** Campaign-length; arcs are organised around vows, which end when fulfilled (the rulebook also has a move for abandoning a vow; not verified in this pass).

### Additional example 2: *80 Days* (inkle, 2014)

Why chosen: a digital game where the text the player reads **is** a written record of the journey, and where choice is frequent, small and always moving forward. It answers the "keep the story moving" question directly.

- **Who directs.** The player, as Passepartout, chooses routes across a globe and choices within each scene. [primary: [inkle launch post](https://www.inklestudios.com/2014/07/31/out-today)]
- **What it tracks and offers.** inkle describes it as "every line drawn on the globe is a journey, and every journey has a story, and every story has choices, and every choice is remembered". The text is Passepartout's journal. [primary: [inkle launch post](https://www.inklestudios.com/2014/07/31/out-today); secondary: [Digital Trends](https://www.digitaltrends.com/gaming/80-days-review/)] Ingold's post-mortem describes it as "a board-game that narrates itself", with narrative adapting to game circumstances. [primary: [GDC Vault, 80 DAYS Post-mortem](https://gdcvault.com/play/1021666/80-DAYS-Post-mortem-Letting)] His GDC 2015 talk covers how inkle makes choices matter and how they "pace and coerce" narrative. [primary: [GDC 2015 talk (Internet Archive)](https://archive.org/details/GDC2015Ingold); secondary summary: [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/video-innovating-interactive-fiction-with-i-80-days-i-jon-ingold)]
- **Coherence.** Ingold's later talk on *Sorcery!* (same studio, same approach) sorts what a scene must check into world state, the player's knowledge, and the recent past, so ad hoc encounters still make sense in any order. [primary: [GDC Vault, Narrative Sorcery](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1023989/Narrative-Sorcery-Coherent-Storytelling-in); [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/narrative-sorcery-coherent-storytelling-in-an-open-world)]
- **Length and ending.** A full trip is roughly 1 to 4 hours; it ends on return to London, win or lose against the 80-day wager, and the game does not cut off a late trip. About 150 cities give a different story on replay. [secondary: [Giant Bomb](https://www.giantbomb.com/games/3030-47793/); [Pure Nintendo](https://purenintendo.com/review-80-days-nintendo-switch/)]

### Additional example 3: *Caves of Qud* (Freehold Games), generated histories

Why chosen: a systemic game, close to Dwarf Fortress in audience, whose designers published how they generate a **retellable** history and fold it into the player's journal.

- **What it does.** Generates mythic biographies of historical "sultans" by first generating events and then rationalising them after the fact, using a state machine and a replacement grammar, deliberately subverting cause and effect. [primary: Grinblat and Bucklew, "Subverting historical cause & effect", FDG 2017, [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3102071.3110574); [PDF](https://www.pcgworkshop.com/archive/grinblat2017subverting.pdf)] Grinblat also wrote the "Generating Histories" chapter in *Procedural Storytelling in Game Design*. [primary: [Taylor and Francis](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.1201/9780429488337-18/generating-histories-jason-grinblat)]
- **Relevance.** It shows a history can be *made coherent after the fact* rather than simulated causally. That is closer to what a narrating LLM does than Dwarf Fortress's simulation is.
- Gap: the in-game journal and quest system were not verified in this pass. The companion paper on quests is [Warm Rocks for Cold Lizards](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-2862/paper5.pdf) [primary], not read.

## Across the precedents

### Tracking devices

| Device | Example | Who writes it | What it does for coherence | What it does for retelling |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| None (the player's head or notebook) | *Maze*, *Blue Prince* | Player | Low; the player must reconcile | Retelling is personal; the notes become the story |
| Full simulation record | DF Legends, world history export | Game | High; nothing is forgotten | Overwhelming; needs curation |
| In-world artefacts | DF engravings | Game (characters) | Echoes past events back into play | Gives the player found reminders |
| Running journal as the text itself | *80 Days* | Game, in a character's voice | High; "every choice is remembered" | The journal *is* the retelling |
| Bounded memory plus diary | *Thousand Year Old Vampire* | Player, under rules | Deliberately lossy | The journal is the artefact; loss is the theme |
| Progress tracks and sheets | *Ironsworn* | Player | Keeps goals visible | Journal is optional, player-kept |
| State checks (world, knowledge, recent past) | inkle's *Sorcery!* | Game, hidden | Keeps ad hoc scenes consistent | Indirect |

The academic thread is consistent. James Ryan argues emergent stories "actually happen", more like non-fiction than fiction, and that the raw trace of a simulation "will almost always lack story structure"; a story needs curation, either by the player or by a system ("curationism"). [primary: Ryan, *Curating Simulated Storyworlds*, PhD thesis, UC Santa Cruz 2018, [eScholarship](https://escholarship.org/uc/item/1340j5h2)] Kreminski and Mateas describe players' retellings as coauthorship: players treat logged events as ground truth but embellish and extrapolate beyond them, and note that games like Dwarf Fortress overwhelm players with information, making the best story threads hard to find. [primary: Kreminski and Mateas, "A Coauthorship-Centric History of Interactive Emergent Narrative", ICIDS 2021, [PDF](https://mkremins.github.io/publications/IENHistory_ICIDS2021.pdf)]

So a record does two jobs that pull against each other: **completeness** (consistency, nothing contradicted) and **selection** (a story worth retelling). Games pick one: Legends picks completeness; TYOV's five Memories pick selection; *80 Days* has an authored voice do the selecting as it goes.

### Branching paths for suggestions

| Game | Form of the offer | Typical count | What forces commitment |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Maze* | Doors in a picture | a handful per room | Nothing but the 16-step puzzle goal |
| *Blue Prince* | Draft one of three rooms behind a door | 3 | 50 steps a day; gems and keys |
| *Dwarf Fortress* | No offer; the world presents problems | open | Survival pressure |
| *TYOV* | Dice pick a prompt | 1 prompt, picked by dice | The player cannot choose the prompt |
| *Ironsworn* | Player asks; oracle answers or sparks | 1 answer to the player's question | Odds the player set; vows |
| *80 Days* | A few text choices per scene; routes on a map | small | 80-day clock, money |

Observations:

- **Small menus are the norm.** Nobody offers an open list. Three is common (Blue Prince drafts; TYOV prompts per page).
- **Momentum comes from a budget, not from removing choice.** Steps, days, dice and a clock make each choice cost something, so the player keeps moving. Maze, with no budget, is the one that lets readers loop forever.
- **Two directions of suggestion.** Most games *offer* options (Blue Prince, *80 Days*). Ironsworn *answers* the player: the player proposes, the oracle rules. TYOV *imposes* a prompt and lets the player decide its meaning.
- **Unreliable suggestion is a choice too.** Maze's Guide may mislead, which makes the reader read the offer critically rather than follow it.

### Story length and structure

| Game | Session | Run | How it ends |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Maze* | One sitting or many | Open | Reader stops, or solves |
| *Blue Prince* | One or more in-game days | Many days | Day: steps run out or player ends it. Game: Room 46, then post-game |
| *Dwarf Fortress* | Hours | In-game years per fortress; worlds persist | Fortress lost or abandoned; history persists |
| *TYOV* | 1 to 4 hours | Hours to weeks | A prompt brings the end; player narrates it |
| *Ironsworn* | A session | Campaign | Arcs end with vows (fulfilled or abandoned) |
| *80 Days* | One sitting | About 1 to 4 hours | Return to London, win or lose |

Patterns: a **short loop inside a long arc** (Blue Prince day inside the hunt; DF fortress inside a world); **endings that tolerate failure** (DF, TYOV, 80 Days); and **persistence across runs** in the systemic games (Blue Prince upgrades and player knowledge; DF worlds).

## Observations for Narinav (not decisions)

These are notes for later tickets. None of them is a decision.

- In every precedent the game's response is bounded. The analogue for Claude's offers would be a small number of options or a single answer to the player's own question, not open-ended generation.
- The Ironsworn oracle shape (player asks, system answers with stated odds) and the *Maze* Guide shape (a narrator with its own voice, possibly unreliable) are two distinct roles Claude could play.
- Ryan and Kreminski suggest the record of play and the retold story are different things. A narrator that writes the record in a voice as it goes (as in *80 Days*) collapses them; a full log plus later curation (as in DF Legends) keeps them apart.
- TYOV shows that a deliberately limited memory can be a feature rather than a technical limitation.
- Blue Prince's lack of an in-game journal is criticised by a reviewer but also leaves note-taking to players who enjoy it. This is relevant for the Blue Prince and Dwarf Fortress audience.
- Every precedent with momentum has a budget. What Narinav's budget is (if any) is an open question.
